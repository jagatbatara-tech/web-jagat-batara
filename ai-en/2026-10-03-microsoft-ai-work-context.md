While studying AB-731, I came across a question I found quite interesting. What baffled me was not that Microsoft's AI products were very different, but that they seemed to have so many pieces.

With Microsoft 365 Copilot, I can write documents, analyze data, create scripts, debug, and even use a powerful model like Claude Opus to help with technical work.

That raises a question:

> "If you can do all of that, then what's the point of GitHub Copilot, Copilot Studio, or Azure AI Foundry?"

The more I learned, the more I began to understand that the difference was not in the AI model being used.

Microsoft 365 Copilot might be able to help write code. But GitHub Copilot understands repositories, branches, pull requests, coding standards, and the context of ongoing development.

The same goes for Copilot Studio and Azure AI Foundry. Both can be used to build agents, but the context in which they are used is different. Copilot Studio focuses more on the needs of business users and day-to-day work automation.

Azure AI Foundry, meanwhile, is designed to build, evaluate, manage, and operate AI solutions at enterprise scale.

So the question is no longer:

> "Which AI is smarter?"

Instead:

> "Which AI has a more complete work context?"

Here's a simple analogy.

Imagine Claude Opus is the best engineer in the world.

Microsoft 365 Copilot gives us the opportunity to talk to those engineers.

GitHub Copilot puts those same engineers directly inside the workshop, looking at the machines being disassembled, the blueprints, and the work in progress.

Copilot Studio gives us the toolkit to assemble our own work assistants without having to be an engineer.

Azure AI Foundry gives us access to a "factory" where AI systems are designed, tested, secured, and operated at scale.

The engineers are the same. The context is different.

And maybe that's where I started to understand Microsoft's AI ecosystem.

It's not a matter of which AI is the smartest, but which AI has the most relevant context for the work we're doing.
