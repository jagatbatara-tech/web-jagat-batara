1
Lesson 1 · Context

Understand the context before you touch a chart

Success starts before the visualization. Know who decides, what you want them to do, and which data proves it.

Exploratory vs. explanatory analysis

Exploratory work is how you find out what is interesting. Explanatory work is how you help someone else understand one thing you already know. The book's image: open 100 oysters to find two pearls, then show your audience the pearls, not the oysters.

ExploratoryExplanatory
GoalFind out what might be interestingExplain one specific point
AudienceYou and fellow analystsA named decision maker
OutputMany quick, rough viewsOne or two polished views with a message
Typical mistakeSending the rough views to the bossHiding the conclusion and making them re-discover it
In logisticsA pivot table with 40 filtersA weekly page that opens with "what changed, what to do"

Who, What and How

1

Who

Be specific about the audience. "Internal and external stakeholders" is not an audience; "the Operations Director who signs the lease" is. Name the decision maker, and think about whether they already trust you as an expert.

2

What

State what they should know or do: approve, fund, stop, escalate, change. If you cannot name an action, ask whether you need to communicate at all. If you present only data, people say "interesting" and move on.

3

How

Only now ask which data supports the point. The data is evidence for the story, not the story. Do not hide evidence that cuts the other way; a sharp reader will find it and doubt everything else.

Mechanism and tone

Live talk

You control pace and attention, and you are there to answer questions. Keep slides sparse and do not use them as a teleprompter.

Slideument

One file serving both a talk and a read-alone document. It satisfies neither fully; later lessons show ways around it.

Written / dashboard

The reader controls pace and nobody is there to explain, so titles, labels and notes must do the talking.

Also decide the tone: celebrating a win, lighting a fire, or reporting calmly. Tone drives later choices such as colour and layout.

Application note

A self-serve dashboard sits at the "written" end. Nobody is in the room, so each page needs a headline that says what to conclude, not just a topic label.

The Big Idea and the 3-minute story

The 3-minute story is what you would say if you only had three minutes. The Big Idea shrinks it to one sentence. Being short is harder than being long, and it frees you from depending on slides.

A Big Idea must (1) state your unique point of view, (2) say what is at stake, and (3) be a complete sentence.

Example (fictional)

"Storage demand will outgrow our current warehouse by year 3. We recommend securing additional space now, because waiting puts projects at risk; please approve the lease study."

Point of view: lease now. Stake: projects at risk. Complete sentence with an ask.

Questions to ask whoever requested the work

  • What background information is essential?
  • Who is the audience or decision maker, and what do we know about them?
  • What biases might make them supportive of, or resistant to, the message?
  • What data would strengthen the case, and is it familiar to them?
  • Where are the risks that could weaken the case?
  • What does a successful outcome look like?
  • If you had one sentence, what would you say?

Storyboard on paper first

Do not start in presentation software; it pulls you into slide-making mode and makes you attached to what you built. Use sticky notes or paper so ideas are cheap to move or bin. Here is a storyboard for the fictional warehouse case.

1 · ContextWhy we forecast space: build or lease decisions are costly and hard to reverse.
2 · ProblemForecast demand crosses current capacity in year 3.
3 · EvidenceOne line chart: forecast vs capacity, gap shaded.
4 · OptionsLease now, build later, or raise utilization.
5 · AskApprove a lease study this quarter.

Busy audience that already trusts you? Move note 5 to the front and lead with the ask.

Lesson 2 · Choose a visual

Pick the chart your audience can read fastest

A dozen visual types cover nearly every business need. The right one is whichever is easiest for your audience to read.

Tables interact with our verbal system: we read them. Graphs interact with our visual system, which is faster. That one idea explains most of the advice below.

Choose by the message, not by the tool

Try it: Chart Picker

Choose what you want to say. The picker recommends a visual and tells you what to avoid.

Four visuals to retire

Pie chart

Eyes cannot judge angles and areas well. When slices are close, you cannot rank them at all.

Swap: sorted horizontal bars. Try it in Lab B.

Donut chart

It asks readers to compare arc lengths, which is harder still than a pie.

Swap: a bar, or just the number.

3D

Use 3D only if you truly plot a third dimension. Otherwise it skews values; in the book a bar of 1 reads as about 0.8.

Swap: flat 2D.

Secondary y-axis

Readers must work out which axis each series uses, and the pairing implies a relationship that may not exist.

Swap: direct labels or two panels sharing the x-axis. Try Lab C.

If you still want a pie

Pause and ask why. If you can answer, label the values and keep it flat. It should never be the first chart you reach for.

Rules for the two workhorses

Bar charts

  • Zero baseline, always. Eyes compare bar ends, so a truncated axis fakes a difference. Test it in Lab A.
  • Bars wider than the gap between them, but not so wide that readers compare areas.
  • Natural order if one exists (age bands, months); otherwise sort, biggest or most important first.
  • Horizontal bars when labels are long: text reads left to right and the label comes before the data.
  • Stacked bars: only the bottom segment shares a baseline, so put the series you care about there.

Line charts

  • Use for continuous data, usually time. The line implies the points are connected.
  • Keep time steps even. Mixing decades and single years distorts the shape.
  • A non-zero baseline is allowed, but say so and avoid over-zooming tiny changes.
  • Show forecast as a thinner dotted line with light shading, never as the same solid line as actuals.
  • Label lines directly instead of using a legend.

Tables

Great for mixed audiences who each look up their own row, or when units differ. Let the data lead: light or no borders, right-aligned numbers, consistent decimals. In live talks, a table pulls attention away from you.

Axis or data labels?

If the big-picture trend matters, keep a light grey axis. If exact values matter, label the data points and drop the axis, because showing both is redundant.

Ethics

Manipulating a scale because it supports your point is not acceptable, and it is risky: one discerning reader spots it and your credibility goes with it.

Lesson 3 · Clutter

Clutter is your enemy

Every element you add costs your audience brain power. Keep what carries information; delete what does not.

Cognitive load

The mental effort needed to take in new information. Extraneous load is effort that does not help understanding.

Perceived load

What matters most is how hard your audience believes it will be. Decided in a glance, often unconsciously. If it looks hard, many will not try.

Signal vs. noise

Signal is the message; noise is anything that does not add to it, or detracts. Maximize the share of ink spent on data.

Gestalt principles: how people group what they see

These six principles tell you what viewers will group together, and therefore which extra elements you can safely remove.

Proximity

Things close together belong together. Use: group related KPIs; put labels next to the data they describe.

Similarity

Same colour, shape or size means related. Use: colour a label like its line; no borders needed to show rows.

Enclosure

Things inside a boundary form a group, even a faint one. Use: light shading to separate forecast from actuals.

Closure

We fill gaps to see a whole shape. Use: delete chart borders and background fills; the chart still looks complete.

Continuity

Eyes follow the smoothest path. Use: drop the vertical axis line; aligned labels and bars imply it.

Connection

Connected things are grouped, even across colours. Use: lines in a line chart link points into a series.

Visual order: alignment, white space, contrast

Alignment

Create clean lines. Left-align text; centre-aligned blocks leave ragged edges and look sloppy. Put the title, axis title and legend top-left so readers learn how to read before they see data. Avoid diagonal text: one study the book cites found 45° text about 52% slower to read and 90° text about 205% slower.

White space

People fear empty space, but it works like a pause in a talk. Keep margins clear, do not stretch charts to fill the page, and never add data just because there is room. If one thing matters most, make it the only thing on the page.

Contrast

It is easy to spot a hawk in a sky of pigeons, and hard when there are many kinds of birds. The more things you make different, the less any one stands out. Use contrast only for the point.

Lesson 4 · Focus

Focus your audience's attention

Use size, colour and position sparingly so people look at what matters before they know they are looking.

How people see and remember

Iconic memory

Lasts a fraction of a second and is tuned to differences. This is where preattentive attributes act.

Short-term memory

Holds about four chunks at once. Ten coloured series plus a legend overload it. Label directly and group information into larger chunks.

Long-term memory

Pictures help recall words, and repetition helps things stick. Pair visuals with words.

Preattentive attributes

These are features the brain notices without effort. Some can encode quantity; others only separate categories.

AttributeQuantity or category?Dashboard use
Position, line lengthQuantity can encode valueWhy bars and lines are easy to read.
Line width, size, colour intensityQuantity to a limited extentSize signals importance; saturation makes a heatmap.
Colour hue, shape, orientation, curvature, enclosureCategorySeparate series or highlight one; "which is greater, red or blue?" is meaningless.
Added marks (labels, markers, underline)Category"Look here" signals, for example a marker and label on the last point.
Bold, italics, underline (text)CategoryBold is the cleanest; italics are mild; underline adds noise.
Motion, blinkingAvoidFlashing is more annoying than helpful. In a live talk, a simple appear/disappear build can guide attention; avoid fly-ins and fades.

The recipe: everything grey, then one accent

  1. Push everything to the background. Grey axes, grey labels, grey context series.
  2. Make the data stand out. Data bolder and larger than axes and labels.
  3. Choose the one thing. One series, one bar or one quadrant carries the accent colour.
  4. Add marks sparingly. Label only the points that tell the story; labelling all of them creates a cluttered mess.
  5. Say it in words. Make the title name what to look at, and match its colour to the data it describes.
The "where are your eyes drawn?" test

Finish the visual, look away, look back, and note where your eyes land first. Ask a colleague to talk you through it. If they land elsewhere, change the emphasis.

Highlighting has a cost

Highlighting one point makes the others harder to see, so avoid it during exploration. Also keep highlighted elements to a small fraction of the design; the book cites a guideline of at most about 10%.

Colour rules

Use sparingly and consistently

A rainbow means nothing stands out. Keep one scheme across the whole communication so readers learn that, say, copper means "look here". Change colour only to signal a real change. If regions have colours, keep them the same everywhere.

Grey base
Accent
Positive / info
Problem

Design for colour blindness

Roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women have trouble with red and green. Avoid putting them side by side, or add a second cue such as bold, saturation, or a + / − sign. Blue and orange are a safe positive/negative pair.

Mind the tone

Colour sets the mood. A clinical report and a light feature need different palettes. Check cultural meanings of colours for international audiences.

Brand colours

Pick one or two brand colours as your "look here" cues and keep the rest grey. If the brand colour is too pale to stand out, use bold black or another colour that does not clash.

Size and position

Size = importance

Give equal-importance items equal size. The book recounts a dashboard where the data that happened to be available took about 60% of the space and nearly steered the decision. Make design a decision, not an accident.

Position = reading order

Without cues, readers start top-left and zigzag in a "Z". Put the most important thing top-left, and do not fight habits such as negative on the left, positive on the right.

Lesson 5 · Think like a designer

Make it obvious how to read it

Form follows function: decide what the audience must do with the data, then design so they can do it with ease.

Affordances: the visual says how to use it

A door handle shows you to pull. A good visual shows you where to look. Three moves create that.

1

Highlight the important stuff

Layer size, colour and bold on the one thing that matters. Keep the highlighted share small.

2

Eliminate distractions

Ask of every item: would removing this change anything? If not, remove it. Summarize detail the audience does not need; move the rest to an appendix.

3

Create a visual hierarchy

Order things by importance with size, colour and position. Super-categories (for example Age / Region / Income) give readers a frame.

Accessibility: usable by people with different skills

Do not overcomplicate

Research the book cites found that instructions in a fussy font were judged harder, and people were less likely to try them. The more complicated it looks, the less likely people are to spend time on it.

  • Use one legible font and size.
  • Prefer simple words; spell out acronyms on first use.
  • When unsure, choose the simpler option.
  • If readers struggle, the design is at fault, not the reader.

Text is your friend

Every chart needs a title and every axis needs a title. State the conclusion in words, because two people can read the same chart and conclude differently. Annotate to explain a spike, a policy change or an outage.

Rare exception: when you want readers to focus on peaks and valleys rather than the exact metric, you may drop the vertical axis labels deliberately.

Action titles beat topic labels

The title is prime real estate. Use it for the conclusion or the ask.

Descriptive (topic label)Action title (conclusion)
Inbound OTIF by weekInbound OTIF fell below the 90% target in 3 of the last 4 weeks
Warehouse utilizationZones A and C exceed the 85% planning ceiling; B and D have room
Vessel waiting time, Q3Vessel waiting time doubled in Q3, mostly because the berth was occupied

Aesthetics: pretty is functional

People rate attractive designs as easier to use, accept them more readily and tolerate their flaws. Three habits create it without being a designer: use colour deliberately, align elements on clean lines, and use white space on purpose. When you meet a chart you like, ask why, and keep a library to borrow from.

Winning acceptance for a new design

Familiarity breeds comfort, so even better designs meet resistance. Four tactics help.

Explain the benefit

Say why it will look different and what readers gain.

Show side by side

A before and after makes the improvement visible.

Offer options

Create two or three versions and ask for input.

Recruit an ally

Win over an influential reader one to one.

If resistance persists, test with someone neutral: the problem may be your design, not their habits.

Lesson 6 · Tell a story

Give the data a beginning, a middle and an end

Stories stick in ways facts alone do not. Build tension between what is and what could be, then end with a clear call to action.

The three-act structure applied to a data story

Beginning · Plot

Set the stage and answer "why should I care?" Five questions help:

  • Setting: when and where?
  • Main character: your audience
  • Imbalance: what changed?
  • Balance: what should happen?
  • Solution: how do we get there?
Middle · Twists

Build the case for "what could be":

  • Background and external comparisons
  • Examples and data that show the problem
  • What happens if nothing changes
  • Options and the benefits of your pick
  • Why they are the right people to act
End · Call to action

Say exactly what you want them to do. A classic close ties back to the opening problem and restates the urgency.

Why bullets alone fail

A deck of bulleted facts persuades only intellectually, and the audience argues with you in their heads. A story joins an idea to an emotion and gives people a reason to act. If you think "I have no problem to frame", look again: conflict is what holds attention, and a story where all is well rarely moves anyone.

Narrative flow: choose the order

OrderWorks best when…How
ChronologicalYou must build credibility, or the audience cares about your methodProblem → data → analysis → finding → recommendation
Lead with the endingThe audience already trusts you, is busy, or wants the "so what"Start with the ask, then back up with the strongest evidence

Tell a live audience which structure you are using ("I will start with what I need from you"). In a written report, the words on each page must make the point on their own.

Four tactics to check the story is clear

Horizontal logic

Read only the slide or section titles. Together they should tell the whole story. This needs action titles. An executive summary up front, mirrored by the titles that follow, makes the path visible.

Vertical logic

Everything on one page reinforces everything else: title supports visual, words support chart, nothing unrelated. Cutting or moving to an appendix is as important as adding.

Reverse storyboarding

Flip through the finished deck and write the main point of each page. The list should look like your original storyboard. If not, move, add or remove pages.

A fresh perspective

Give it to someone with no context and ask what they notice, what matters and what is unclear. You are too close to see it as your audience does.

Repetition: Bing, Bang, Bongo

1

Bing

Tell them what you are about to tell them: an executive summary.

2

Bang

Tell them: the detailed content.

3

Bongo

Tell them what you told them: recap, with the action you need.

Short, repeatable sound bites help your message travel without you.

    Practice · What-if Lab

    Change one thing and watch the story change

    The data stays the same in every lab. Only the design choices move, which shows how much power the designer holds, and the responsibility that comes with it.

    Lab A · Where does the axis start? Lesson 2

    Five hubs hit between 93.8% and 96.1% on-time delivery. Slide the baseline of the bar chart and compare what the eye sees with what the numbers say.

    Lab B · Pie or bar? Lesson 2

    Enter the share of delays by cause (any numbers; they are converted to percentages). Try to rank the pie slices, then look at the bars.

    Lab C · Same data, two stories on a dual axis Lessons 2, 4

    Which data actually belongs on the dashboard?

    For each scenario, choose what to show, pick the main visual, and pick the headline. The hardest part of a good dashboard is leaving things out.
    How scoring works

    Each exercise scores 100 points: 50 for the data you pick, 25 for the visual and 25 for the title. For data, each core item you include earns full credit, supporting items earn half credit, and noise or misleading items cost points. Missing a core item costs you the points that item would have earned.

    Apply · Toolkit

    Take it back to your own dashboards

    Use a layout that tells the story in layers, run the pre-flight checklist, then storyboard on paper before you open your tool.

    Anatomy of a story-first dashboard page

    1. Headline. The conclusion or the ask, top-left, where eyes land first.
    2. One to three KPIs with a target and a trend. Only the one needing attention gets the accent colour.
    3. Main visual. Answers "why", with grey context and one highlighted series.
    4. Secondary visual. Answers "where" or "who".
    5. Detail on demand. Definitions, tables and filters for people who want to verify.
