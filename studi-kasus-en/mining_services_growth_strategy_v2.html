Where should a mining contractor grow when its core market is under pressure?

Fix the cost base first, win the contracts that are actually open, and enter adjacent services through partnerships, in a set order. Cost is the one lever a contractor controls: in 2013 the largest players dug more coal, yet each tonne carried less work, and by December 2014 the benchmark coal price was 43% below its March 2012 level.

The core is squeezed

Lower prices and lower strip ratios cut what owners will pay and how much earth-moving work each tonne creates.

Cost is the lever

Contractors do not set prices or strip ratios. About 70% of their operating cost sits in two lines they can manage.

Growth must be accessible

Chase contracts that are opening, services buyers can unbundle, and adjacencies in order of capability fit, with partners where capital is short.

The core business is squeezed from three sides at once

The client had to grow, yet the usual route, more fleet and more contracts, was getting less profitable. Three forces explain why.

Prices fell

The benchmark price dropped from US$112.87 per tonne in March 2012 to US$64.65 by December 2014. Owners passed the pressure on as rate cuts.

Less work per tonne

Owners cut strip ratios to lower their own cost. Overburden removal is a key contractor revenue driver, so revenue per tonne of coal fell.

Entrenched rivals

About 116 contractors compete, but the top seven hold roughly 60% of contracted production, often through long contracts and owner affiliation.

What the contractor can and cannot change

Splitting margin into its drivers shows that the biggest pressures sit outside the contractor’s control. What remains is cost per BCM (bank cubic metre of earth moved).

Four hypotheses structured the work

The team converted the problem into testable hypotheses and used interviews to confirm or reject each. The client also set a hard boundary: little capacity for extra investment or staff, so the strategy had to deliver maximum impact with minimum support, starting with quick wins.

H1. Cost leadership should come first

Supported

Owners were cutting strip ratios and pressing rates, while leading peers reported around 22% operating margin and cost cuts of up to 12% (about US$31M) over 2013 to 2014.

H2. Open contracts can be predicted

Supported

Windows opened at contract expiry, incumbent underperformance, or new concessions beyond incumbent capacity. Owner affiliation was the strongest predictor of who held the work.

H3. Differentiation needs full pit-to-port ownership

Partially rejected

Buyers could procure drilling and blasting, hauling, and ports separately, and specialists had succeeded by focusing on one segment. With limited capital, partnership beat ownership.

H4. Construction is the logical adjacency

Supported, in sequence

Only two of the six largest contractors had meaningful construction exposure. Mining infrastructure transfers best; piling and EPC need partners, references, and engineering depth.

Evidence base: 50+ stakeholders (3+ experts and associations, 10+ mine owners, 15+ contractors, 5+ traders, 10+ adjacent-industry companies, 5 to 10 construction participants), plus secondary research and competitor benchmarking.

Cost per BCM is the one lever in the contractor’s hands

Cost leadership does two jobs at once. It protects margin when owners cut rates, and it lets the contractor quote lower than rivals when bidding for new contracts. The shift is in the operating objective: from the highest production to production at the lowest cost per BCM.

Grow where the contractor can win without a price war or a large cheque

Every option was screened on seven questions: Is the market attractive? Do capabilities transfer? Can the client reach the customer? How much capital is needed? How fast does it pay back? Can it win beyond price? What is missing to execute?

1. Pursue contracts that are opening, not every concession

2. Unbundle the pit-to-port chain and focus on three links

Buyers can purchase services separately. The question is which links are worth entering. The answer: those where rivals are not already fighting on price, and where a partner can share the capital.

3. Enter adjacent markets in order of capability fit

Six pathways in a set order keep the core strong before anything new is funded

The order follows speed and effort: quick, low-capital moves first, anything that needs new capabilities last.

How the strategy could be governed

In hindsight, the squeeze thesis held through 2017, but a single-point forecast missed the upswing

This section is an observation made years later, not a claim about project impact. It compares the base-case forecast in the original analysis with what the data later showed.

  • The squeeze was real. Output sat near 456 to 461 Mt from 2014 to 2017 while the benchmark price fell to US$53.51 by December 2015. That is the environment in which cost leadership first was the right call.
  • The forecast carried no range. The single base case of 504 Mt for 2020 was 12% below the 564 Mt that followed. Next time I would present scenario bands, so the plan holds up in both a squeeze and an upswing.
  • Early data deserves a caveat. The 2013 figure used then (421 Mt) was later revised to about 474 Mt. Flag data vintage when a fact base is built on preliminary numbers.

What a reader should take away, and what this case does not claim

  • Fix the core before diversifying. Expansion is not credible while cost, maintenance, and fleet productivity are unresolved.
  • Screen for accessibility, not only attractiveness. Contract expiry, affiliation, and incumbent performance can matter more than market size.
  • Integration is not ownership. Partnerships, unbundled services, and operational JVs can lower capital exposure.
  • Sequence capability building. Mining infrastructure sits closer to the existing base than structural steel or full EPC.
  • Separate outputs from outcomes. A decision-ready strategy is a valid consulting result, but it is not realized financial impact.
Result integrity. The engagement produced strategic outputs and decision value. It did not document realized revenue or cost savings after implementation, so none are claimed here.
