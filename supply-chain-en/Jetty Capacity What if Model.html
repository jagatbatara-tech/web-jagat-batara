When Does Jetty Congestion Constrain Capacity?

A berth is not full at 100%. Vessels start queueing long before that. This page explains the method, gives every formula, takes your full set of parameters, and shows when congestion bites and what an additional jetty or ship-to-ship (STS) transfer can do about it.

The short version

Six ideas behind this model

01

Occupancy is not congestion. Berth occupancy (BOR) says how full the berth is. Waiting time says how much queueing it causes. Waiting rises steeply well before 100%.

02

Variability matters as much as volume. Irregular arrivals and uneven berth times create queues even when average capacity looks adequate.

03

More berths change the tipping point. Two berths tolerate a higher average occupancy than one, because a free berth can take the next vessel.

04

STS relieves the berth, not the system. Mother vessels wait at anchorage, and lighters still need somewhere to discharge. The model loads both.

05

Capacity is what you can use. The model finds the tipping BOR where average wait reaches your threshold, and treats that as effective capacity.

06

Every number is yours. The example values are generic placeholders. Replace them with your own vessel-call history.

1
The question

Why a berth is “full” long before 100%

A berth occupancy ratio tells you how much of the year a berth is busy. It does not tell you how long a vessel waits for it, and waiting is what costs money.

Berth occupancy is usually calculated as vessel calls × berth time ÷ available berth time. At 100% the berth has no free time at all, so any occupancy figure above 100% is physically impossible. But vessels do not arrive on a perfect timetable, and they do not take identical time at the berth. Random gaps and overlaps create a queue at anchorage.

That queue grows slowly at low occupancy and then sharply. Analytical work on container terminals relates berth occupancy to the ratio of waiting time to service time, and notes that the berths required depend on the distributions of ship arrivals and service times, including peak factors (Radmilović and Jovanović, 2006). The tipping point depends on the number of berths, how regular the arrivals are, and how uneven berth times are. This model calculates it for your own numbers.

Many BOR models also count only port stay, from berthing to departure. Waiting at anchorage before berthing then sits outside the BOR figure, so a berth can report a comfortable BOR while vessels queue outside.

Three facilities, one logic

  • Main berth(s): where every vessel goes if you do nothing.
  • Additional jetty: a second facility with its own opening year, availability, and productivity relative to the main berth. It takes the share of each cargo class you assign.
  • STS anchor spot(s): mother vessels discharge to lighters at anchorage. It has its own rates and a days-based capacity, and the lighters may add berth time at a jetty.

Each facility is its own queue. The model compares “do nothing” against your plan year by year, then finds the tipping point and the waiting cost.

The honest scope

This is a planning model on annual averages with a standard queueing approximation. It tells you when congestion is likely to constrain capacity and roughly how much relief an option gives. It does not replace a berth-scheduling simulation.

Formulas

Every calculation, in order

Symbols: i = cargo class, y = year, f = facility (main, additional jetty, STS).

1Vessel calls

N(i,y) = ROUND( Volume(i,y) ÷ TonsPerCall(i) ) [if volume is entered in tons] N(i,y) = Volume(i,y) [if volume is entered as vessels]

Rounding can be nearest, upward, or none (fractional calls). “Nearest” is common in spreadsheet models. “None” gives smoother what-if curves.

2Berth hours per call, by handling mode

Container: H = (TonsPerCall ÷ TonsPerBox) × (1 + Backload) ÷ BoxesPerHour Piece: H = (TonsPerCall ÷ TonsPerPiece) ÷ PiecesPerHour Bulk: H = TonsPerCall ÷ TonsPerHour Fixed: H = 0 (use the extra fixed hours, for example a historical median port stay) Total hours per call = H + ExtraFixedHours

Backload counts the empty boxes returned for each full box as extra lifts. Rates should be productivity while working, not averages that already include waiting.

3Berth time, buffer, and BOR

BerthHours(f) = Σ N × H Buffer(f) = Σ N × TurnaroundHours + BufferPct × BerthHours TotalHours(f) = BerthHours + Buffer Capacity(f) = Berths × 365 × Availability [days per year] BOR(f) = TotalHours ÷ 24 ÷ Capacity(f)

The turnaround buffer is the idle time between one vessel leaving and the next berthing. “Base BOR” excludes it, “buffered BOR” includes it. Status bands (editable) label the buffered BOR.

4Queue at a facility

ρ = BOR(f) utilization (must be below 1) E[S] = TotalHours ÷ N ÷ Availability mean service time per call (h) Cs² = ( Σ p·s²·(1+CVw²) ) ÷ E[S]² − 1 variability of berth time from the cargo mix a = c × ρ offered load, c = number of berths Erlang B: B(0)=1; B(k) = a·B(k−1) ÷ ( k + a·B(k−1) ) for k = 1…c Erlang C: P(wait) = B(c) ÷ ( 1 − ρ·(1 − B(c)) ) Wq = P(wait) ÷ ( c·(1 − ρ) ) × E[S] × ( Ca² + Cs² ) ÷ 2 average wait before berthing (h)

p = share of calls in each class, s = its service time, CVw = variation of berth time inside a class, Ca² = squared variability of arrival gaps (1 = random arrivals, lower = scheduled). Wq is the Erlang-C waiting time scaled by (Ca² + Cs²) ÷ 2, the Allen–Cunneen approximation. For one berth with random arrivals it equals the Pollaczek–Khinchine result. If ρ ≥ 1 the queue is unstable and wait is unbounded.

5Tipping point and effective capacity

Find ρ* such that Wq(ρ*) = Threshold (solved numerically, bisection) Tipping BOR = ρ* k* = ρ* ÷ ρ Effective capacity = k* × N vessel calls per year at the threshold Nominal capacity = N ÷ ρ calls per year at BOR = 100% Headroom = k* − 1 (negative means congestion already constrains) Years of headroom = ln(k*) ÷ ln(1 + growth)

“Congestion constrains capacity” means average waiting exceeds your threshold, or the queue is unstable. The model also reports a peak-month view: ρ(peak) = ρ × PeakFactor.

6Additional jetty

Calls to jetty = N × Share(i,y) (shares sum to at most 100% with STS) Hours per call at jetty = ProductiveHours ÷ ProductivityFactor + ExtraFixedHours Then repeat steps 3 to 5 for the jetty with its own berths, availability, and opening year.

7STS transfer (days-based, with lighters)

STS hours per mother-vessel call = ProductiveHours(at STS rate) + ExtraFixedHours [or your own override] STS days occupied = Σ N(STS) × hours ÷ 24 STS capacity = AnchorSpots × 365 × Availability STS utilization = DaysOccupied ÷ Capacity (queue at anchorage from step 4) Main-berth relief = removal of N(STS) × H(main) and its buffer Lighter discharge ashore (optional): N(STS) × Trips calls of ShoreHours each, added to the jetty you choose

The optional lighter-cycle helper in the setup section converts lighter capacity, loading rate, sailing time, shore unloading rate, and the number of lighters into mother-vessel hours and shore berth hours.

8Waiting cost

WaitingVesselDays = N × Wq ÷ 24 WaitingCost = WaitingVesselDays × DelayCostPerVesselDay

Compare the “do nothing” cost with the plan to see what congestion costs, then set that against the cost of the option. Capex is not modeled.

Jetty and STS options

If the main berth runs short: three levers

Relief comes from adding capacity, moving load, or reducing variability. The model can test each one.

STS as a bridge, with a catch

STS takes eligible cargo off the main berth. But rates at anchorage are usually lower than at a berth, a mother vessel occupies an anchor spot for longer, and the lighters must discharge somewhere. If they use the same berth, the relief shrinks or disappears. The model therefore tracks the anchor-spot utilization, the queue at anchorage, and the shore berth time the lighters consume.

Weather, swell, and the number of lighters often decide STS productivity more than equipment does.

      Reading the results

      A short decision guide

      1. Check the state, not only the BOR. A BOR below 100% can still be “Constrained”. That is the case the model exists to catch.
      2. Compare tipping BOR with your trigger. If your expansion trigger is 70–80% but the tipping BOR for your mix and berth count is lower, the trigger may fire too late for the waiting level you accept.
      3. Test the cheap levers first. Shorter turnaround, steadier arrivals, and higher handling rate cost less than a new facility. The levers show how far they go.
      4. Use STS as a bridge, then check its limits. Watch the anchor-spot utilization and the lighter discharge load. A high STS utilization, or lighters landing at the same berth, can cancel the relief.
      5. Size an additional jetty by its tipping point. Adding a berth raises the tolerable occupancy, so the capacity gain is larger than its nominal berth-days.
      6. Price the waiting. Set the waiting cost saved against the cost of the option, and test the delay cost and threshold, which are judgment inputs.

      Limits and sources

      What this model cannot do

      It is an approximation

      The queue uses the Erlang C formula scaled by the Allen–Cunneen factor. It treats arrivals as a steady stream over the year, and berths as interchangeable servers. Real berth plans, draft limits, vessel size, tide windows, and cargo-specific berth restrictions make some berths unusable for some calls.

      Waiting is not a forecast

      The result is a long-run average. It does not predict a given week. Use it to compare scenarios, and validate it against recorded anchorage waiting if you have it.

      Variability inputs drive the answer

      Ca² and the berth-time variability are placeholders. Small changes move the tipping point a lot. Calibrate them from your own arrival and port-stay history.

      STS is simplified

      STS uses a days-based capacity and an optional lighter cycle. It ignores swell-driven stoppages beyond the availability input, priority rules, and lighter fleet sharing between mother vessels.

      No capex or schedule detail

      Costs of building a jetty or running STS are not modeled. Berth scheduling, pilotage, tide, and channel constraints are outside scope.

      Your definitions come first

      Define what counts as port stay, waiting, and available time before comparing sites, and use the same rule every year.

      Sources for the method

      • Erlang C (M/M/c) and the Allen–Cunneen approximation for general arrival and service variability: standard queueing theory, widely documented in operations research texts and calculators.
      • Radmilović, Z. and Jovanović, S. (2006), “Berth occupancy at container terminals: comparison of analytical and empirical results”, Promet – Traffic & Transportation 18(2). Relates berth occupancy to waiting and service time ratios, and notes that the berths required depend on arrival and service-time distributions including peak factors.
      • UNCTAD, “Port Development: A Handbook for Planners in Developing Countries” (second edition, 1985). Covers berth occupancy, berth-day requirements, and the number of berths.
      • APICS/ASCM CSCP Learning System 2024, Module 4 (capacity measurement, bottlenecks, balancing load and capacity), used for the capacity framing only.
