Which purchase orders are likely to arrive late?

A purchase order does not become late on its due date. It becomes late earlier, at a hub, while the goods are still waiting for a container or a vessel. This page walks through the physical flow, the KPI at each step, and a forecast model that tells you which orders are drifting late while you can still act.

The short version

Seven ideas behind this model

01

Late is decided at the hub

Once goods are on the water there is little left to do. The useful warning comes while goods sit at the hub, or before the supplier has even delivered.

02

One forecast, two judgements

The same forecast arrival date is compared with the site due date (did the need get met?) and with the freight standard (did logistics do its job?).

03

The clock depends on the stage

Before the hub, the supplier's promise date drives the forecast. At the hub, the standard consolidation. On the water, the actual departure.

04

Tolerance is an escalation window

Each hub has a band of days in which a late-looking order can still be recovered by pushing the departure. Past the band it is late even at standard sailing time.

05

Raw data is per delivery, the order is per line

Each receipt (RTN) is judged on its own for logistics. Quantities are then summed per PO line to decide in full and the site status.

06

The same engine runs outbound

Products leaving the site follow the mirror image: ready date, hub, consolidation, sailing, customer receipt. Only the labels change.

07

Every number is yours

All example values are generic placeholders. Replace them with your own lead times, standards and tolerances before drawing conclusions.

1The question

Which open orders will miss their due date if nothing changes, and which of them can still be saved?

A status such as "Ontime" or "Late" printed after delivery explains the past. A planner needs the opposite: a ranked list of orders that are heading late, a reason, and a date by which action still works. The model on this page builds that list from only five dates per delivery: the supplier promise date, the goods receipt at the hub (GR Offsite), the shipment start, the receipt at site (GR Onsite), and today.

Three questions the page answers. (1) Where in the flow is each order now, and is it drifting? (2) Is the cause the supplier, the hub, or the sailing? (3) If the supplier slips, the hub holds goods longer, or the sailing runs long, how many orders turn late, and which?

Typical causes of delay, by phase (general experience, not company data)

Supplier or plant phase

Production or procurement slips, partial lots, documents not ready, supplier not confirming the date. The order is late before any logistics work starts.

Hub posting phase

Goods stand in the yard or dock waiting for inspection, labelling, or a posting action. The supplier looks late in the system although the goods were physically there.

Consolidation phase

Waiting for other lots to complete a container, for booking space, or for the next sailing. This is the phase where escalation still changes the outcome.

Sailing, clearance and inland phase

Port congestion, transhipment, inspection, customs release, trucking. Little can be recovered once the vessel has left.

4Method

A rule-based forecast, in four steps, applied to every PO line and every receipt.

Step 1. Group the raw data

Raw data has one row per receipt (RTN). If the same PO line appears more than once with different RTNs, the line was delivered in parts. The model keeps both grains.

  • RTN grain carries the logistics assessment, because each delivery has its own dates.
  • PO line grain carries the in-full decision and the site assessment, because quantity is ordered per line.

Step 2. Decide the stage of each PO line

Only events dated on or before the as-of date count. Quantities from all receipts of the line are summed.

StageTest (checked from the bottom up)
ReceivedQuantity at site ≥ PO quantity
On waterQuantity with a shipment start ≥ PO quantity
At hubQuantity posted at the hub ≥ PO quantity (in full at hub)
VendorOtherwise, including partially delivered lines

Step 3. Forecast the arrival

StageForecast arrivalLogic
VendorMAX(as-of, PO Delivery Date) + Lead TimeAssume the supplier delivers on its promise date. Once that date has passed, the earliest possible delivery is today.
At hubAs-of + (Lead Time − Std Consolidation)Best case: the goods leave the hub today. The standard consolidation is already behind them.
On waterShipment Start + (Lead Time − Std Consolidation)The departure date is a fact. Only the standard sailing remains.
ReceivedActual GR OnsiteFinal.
Assumption. Lead Time already includes the standard consolidation at the hub. Due Site Date = PO Delivery Date + Lead Time, and it must not be moved when a supplier delivers early.

Step 4. Compare with the target, then apply the bands

Assessment 1: site status (per PO line)

Target is the Due Site Date. It includes the supplier, so it answers whether the need is met. Only final when the line is in full.

Assessment 2: logistics status (per RTN)

Target is GR Offsite + Lead Time. The clock starts when the goods enter the hub, so the supplier is excluded and each delivery is judged alone.

The tolerance before shipment is used for the vendor and hub stages; the tolerance after shipment is used on the water. Both are variables per hub, separately for the site and the logistics assessment.

5Formulas

Dates and standards

Due Site Date = PO Delivery Date + LT Logistics Target = GR Offsite (this RTN) + LT Remaining LT after hub = LT − Std Consolidation Last departure date = Due Site Date − (LT − Std Consolidation)

Quantities per PO line (events on or before the as-of date)

Qty at hub = Σ RTN qty with GR Offsite ≤ as-of In full at hub = (Qty at hub ≥ PO qty) Qty shipped = Σ RTN qty with Shipment Start ≤ as-of Qty at site = Σ RTN qty with GR Onsite ≤ as-of DIFOT = 1 if received in full and latest GR Onsite ≤ Due Site Date, else 0

Forecast, slack and status

Forecast(Vendor) = MAX(as-of, PO Delivery Date) + LT Forecast(At hub) = as-of + (LT − Std) Forecast(On water) = Shipment Start + (LT − Std) Slack = Target − Forecast (positive = early) Status = band(Slack, tolerance)

Where the days go: exact split of a late arrival

For a received delivery, the days late against the Due Site Date split exactly into four parts, because the Lead Time is the sum of the standard consolidation and the remaining standard:

Arrival − Due Site Date = (3PL receipt − PO Delivery Date) supplier + (GR Offsite − 3PL receipt) GR posting + (Shipment Start − GR Offsite − Std Consolidation) hub dwell vs KPI + (GR Onsite − Shipment Start − (LT − Std)) sailing vs standard

8Reading the results

Do not read "Probably Ontime" as safe

It means the order sits inside the escalation window. With the best-case assumption (depart today), a line with zero slack has no room for any variation. The probability column shows this: lines at zero slack often carry a high probability of late once variation is allowed.

Act on hub lines first

Vendor lines depend on a promise you cannot move. Water lines cannot be changed. Hub lines carry both a date and a lever, so they are the first call for escalation.

Read both statuses together

A line that is late on the site status and on time on logistics is a supplier problem. A line on time at the site but late on logistics is a hidden problem protected by an early supplier.

Test the levers one at a time

A hub hold of seven days and a sailing delay of seven days both add seven days to the arrival of a hub line. They differ in who owns the fix and when you can still act.

Paper 1 · Intelligent Early Warning System for Supplier Delays

Dynamic IoT-calibrated probabilistic modelling in engineer-to-order supply chains.

IdeaCompares a fixed-threshold warning system with an adaptive one. Fixed thresholds keep false alarms low in stable conditions; adaptive thresholds detect better in high-risk conditions.
MappingOur tolerance per hub is the fixed-threshold baseline. The next step is to set it from observed variability of each lane.

Paper 2 · Conformal Predictive Distributions for Order Fulfillment Time Forecasting

Forecasting the distribution of fulfilment time, with a cost rule for decisions.

IdeaStatic table-based rules fail to capture uncertainty. A predicted distribution identifies late shipments better and feeds a cost-based decision rule.
MappingOur freight table is the static baseline. The probability layer in this page is a simple stand-in for a predicted distribution.

Paper 3 · Uncertainty-Aware Delivery Delay Duration Prediction via Multi-Task Deep Learning

Classify delay first, then predict its duration.

IdeaPredicting how long a delivery is late, not only whether it is, supports probabilistic forecasting.
MappingOur four labels correspond to the classification step. The days-late column in section 7 is the duration step in rule-based form.
Reading note: only the abstracts and introductions of these papers were reviewed, and two of them are preprints. Their datasets (engineer-to-order manufacturing, e-commerce, industrial delivery) differ from import purchase orders, so their reported accuracy figures do not transfer to your data.

Back-test routine for upgrade 7

Store one row per PO line per day with its status. When a line closes, compare its status at day T−14 with the outcome:

Hit rate (Probably Late) = lines labelled Probably Late or Late at T−14 that arrived late ÷ lines labelled so Missed late = lines that arrived late but were Ontime or Probably Ontime at T−14 Bias of ETA error = average of (actual arrival − forecast arrival) Tracking signal = cumulative ETA error ÷ MAD investigate when it passes ±4

Recalibrate each tolerance when the hit rate drifts. A lower tolerance raises the share of Late labels and lowers the share of missed late lines; the right value balances false alarms against missed warnings for your business.

Suggested build order

  1. Capture the 3PL physical receipt date and the supplier's confirmed date (small data change, large attribution gain).
  2. Start the daily snapshot now; it takes months to fill and every later upgrade depends on it.
  3. Estimate spreads per route from history and replace the placeholder spreads.
  4. Add the planned departure date at the hub, then vessel ETA.
  5. Rank open lines by risk, value and need date.

11Limits and sources

Limits.
  • All example values (lead times, hubs, tolerances, dates) are generic placeholders, not company data.
  • The forecast is rule-based with fixed standard lead times. It has no vessel ETA, no supplier confirmation date and no lead-time history.
  • The probability layer uses spreads you type in. It is an illustration of the idea, not a calibrated model.
  • A line counts as in full only when the summed quantity reaches the PO quantity. Over-delivery counts as in full; units must match.
  • Latest GR Offsite is used as the in-full date. If an extra receipt arrives after the line is in full, the date moves.
  • Logistics tolerances are placeholders. Not validated on live data.
